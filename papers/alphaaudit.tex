\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A language model that writes trading strategies is a machine for manufacturing plausible
backtests, and the two failure modes that make those backtests worthless---lookahead bias
and multiple testing---are precisely the two a naive evaluation cannot see. \textbf{We release
AlphaAudit: a benchmark for measuring whether an auditor can tell, in advance and out of
sample, which machine-generated strategies are worth acting on.} It comprises a
survivorship-free Indian equity environment, a verified transaction-cost model, leaky and
clean fixture suites, an audited 1,550-candidate corpus, a frozen one-shot evaluation
protocol, and the reference implementation reported below.

\paragraph{Setup.} A local 7B model (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q4\_K\_M}, one fixed prompt,
temperature 0.8) generated $N=1{,}550$ candidate strategies over a survivorship-free,
point-in-time universe of the 100 most liquid NSE equities, rebuilt quarterly from exchange
bhavcopy archives using only prior sessions. Each candidate was executed by a backtester that
raises rather than warns on a stale signal, and priced by a cost model built for the Indian
statutory regime---STT, exchange and IPFT charges, SEBI turnover fee, stamp duty, GST, and a
per-scrip depository charge---applied at the rates in force on each trade's own date. Three
independently ablatable auditors judge each candidate: an AST-based leakage detector, a
statistical layer computing the Deflated Sharpe Ratio against a hash-chained trial ledger the
generator cannot write to, and a local-LLM semantic layer reading each stated rationale against
its own implementation ($\kappa = 0.589$ against 50 human labels).

\keyfinding{\textbf{The dominant failure mode of an LLM strategy researcher is not that it
cheats---it is that it does not run.} All 1,550 draws returned parseable,
interface-conforming code; 631 (40.7\%) survived contact with real data; 406 of those
(64.3\%) never took a position. \textbf{14.5\% of the corpus produced a strategy that both
executes and trades.} The auditor is rarely the binding constraint.}

\paragraph{Costs.} Net of costs, the best of 225 rankable strategies falls from a gross Sharpe of
1.3049 to 1.2807---and survives only because it barely trades. The median strategy loses 0.194 of
Sharpe and 4.4\% of annual return; the mean loses 0.587 and 58.1\%, the gap being a tail churning
above 80$\times$ annual turnover. \textbf{35 of 225 (15.6\%) reverse the sign of their Sharpe, and
11 are bankrupted outright.} None reverse the other way, as none can. Two of eleven standard
academic factors also flip, and momentum, at a net Sharpe of 0.966, no longer beats
equal-weighting the same universe. At an honest trial count of $N=1{,}887$, \textbf{zero of 631
candidates attain a deflated Sharpe probability above 0.95}; the corpus maximum is
$4.4\times10^{-8}$.

\paragraph{The null.} Sweeping coverage from 1.0 to 0.05 over all seven layer combinations, scored
on a holdout evaluated three times under logged authorisation and now permanently closed,
\textbf{no configuration beats random rejection at matched coverage} (AUAP $-1.20$ to $-1.43$
against a random 95\% interval of $[-1.22, -0.86]$; six fall below it). The one configuration that
beat random in sample was the one that provably cannot---its ranking signal is a monotone function
of the quantity it was scored against---and out of sample it fails with the rest. We report the
null rather than tune past it, and note that it survived two subsequent corrections of our own
pipeline---the entire cost implementation, and a sign error in the ranking itself: \emph{the
magnitudes moved, the conclusion did not}. It remains confounded
with corpus degeneracy and cannot separate \textit{``the auditor is uninformative''} from
\textit{``this corpus is too weak to test it''}.

\paragraph{Disposition.} We report the reference implementation's failure as the benchmark's first
entry rather than as a result to be improved upon. The environment, protocol and fixtures are
independent of whether our auditor worked; a stronger generator can be scored against exactly the
same apparatus and contest the second claim directly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ask a large language model for a systematic equity strategy and it will give you one. It
will give you three hundred. Each will arrive with a plausible economic rationale, clean
code, and---if you backtest it naively---a Sharpe ratio that would embarrass most
professional managers. This is not evidence of skill. It is evidence that the search space
is large and that a backtest is a very weak instrument.

Two mechanisms account for nearly all of it. The first is \emph{leakage}: the code sees
information it could not have had. A signal computed from today's close that trades at
today's close is not a strategy, it is a memory. The second is \emph{multiple testing}: if
$N$ independent strategies are tried and the best is selected, the maximum of $N$ draws
from a mean-zero distribution is positive by construction, and grows with $\log N$
\citep{harvey2016,bailey2014}. Neither mechanism announces itself. Both produce equity
curves that look exactly like skill.

An LLM strategy generator makes both worse, and it does so in a specific and
under-appreciated way. It does not merely raise $N$; it raises $N$ silently. A researcher
who tries twenty ideas by hand remembers trying twenty. A generator that emits a candidate
every few seconds, unattended, destroys the researcher's ability to know their own
denominator---and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio is meaningless without an honest one.

This paper builds the instrument, not the strategy. We construct an auditor $A$ that maps
a candidate strategy to a rejection decision, a reason, and a deflated performance
estimate, with three independently ablatable layers: an AST-based static analyser, a
statistical deflation layer backed by a tamper-evident trial ledger, and a local-LLM
semantic layer that reads each strategy's stated rationale against its own implementation.
We then ask whether the auditor is worth having, by measuring \emph{selective deployment}:
if you act only on the fraction $c$ of candidates the auditor most trusts, does realised
out-of-sample performance improve as $c$ falls? The area under that curve, $\mathrm{AUAP}$,
is our primary metric, and the null hypothesis---that $A$ is no better than rejecting at
random---is something we test rather than assume away.

We run the whole apparatus on Indian equities, a market chosen for a reason that turns out
to matter more than expected. Indian statutory transaction costs are heavy, asymmetric
between buy and sell legs, and include a per-scrip depository charge that does not scale
with trade size. A cost model that treats them as a flat basis-point haircut is not a
simplification; it is a different experiment.

\paragraph{What this paper is primarily offering.} The auditor we build fails its own headline
test. We nonetheless regard the paper's central artifact as the \emph{benchmark}, not the
auditor, and we have written it that way. The reason is structural: an auditor is a method and
methods are superseded, but the environment a method must be tested in---a survivorship-free
universe, an honest cost model, a fixed holdout, known-leaky positive controls, and a protocol
that prevents the test set from being optimised against---is infrastructure, and in this area it
does not currently exist openly for any market, let alone for India. Our null is the benchmark's
first entry. It is a low bar, and it is deliberately published as one.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0.2em}
\item \textbf{AlphaAudit, a frozen benchmark for strategy auditing}: environment, fixtures,
  audited corpus, one-shot evaluation protocol, and the AUAP metric with its random-rejection
  baseline (\S\ref{sec:release}).
\item \textbf{A negative result about LLM strategy generation that is not about leakage.}
  Of 1,550 candidates, all 1,550 parsed and conformed to the engine interface; 631 executed
  against real data; 406 of those never traded. The auditor is not the binding
  constraint---the interpreter is (\S\ref{sec:corpus}).
\item \textbf{A time-varying Indian transaction cost model}, verified line by line against a
  discount broker's public calculator, wired \emph{inside} the backtest engine and charged
  per leg. It reverses the sign of 15.6\% of profitable candidates and bankrupts eleven
  (\S\ref{sec:costs-results}).
\item \textbf{An honest trial counter}, hash-chained and append-only, outside the
  generator's write path, and the demonstration that at $N=1{,}887$ nothing in the corpus
  survives deflation (\S\ref{sec:deflation}).
\item \textbf{A pre-registered null, published as the benchmark's reference entry.} The auditor
  does not beat random rejection at matched coverage, in any of seven layer configurations, on a
  holdout evaluated under logged authorisation and now permanently closed. We report it, and we
  report that it survived two full corrections of our own pipeline---the cost implementation, and
  a sign error in the ranking (\S\ref{sec:correction}, \S\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Backtest overfitting.} That the reported Sharpe of a selected strategy is
biased upward is old news; quantifying the bias is not. \citet{bailey2014} derive the
Deflated Sharpe Ratio, which adjusts an observed Sharpe for trial count, sample length,
skewness and kurtosis by raising the threshold a strategy must clear rather than lowering
its estimate. \citet{bailey2017} introduce the Probability of Backtest Overfitting via
combinatorially symmetric cross-validation. \citet{harvey2016} argue that the conventional
$t>2$ hurdle should be roughly $t>3$ once the published factor zoo is treated as one
enormous multiple-testing exercise. \citet{lopezdeprado2018} supplies the machinery we use
for purged $k$-fold cross-validation with embargo. Our contribution is not to these
statistics but to their \emph{plumbing}: all of them require $N$, and none of the papers
that define them address what happens when the researcher is a program that cannot
remember how many times it has tried.

\paragraph{Transaction costs and the anomaly literature.} \citet{novymarx2016} show that a
large share of published anomalies do not survive realistic US trading costs, and that the
survivors are disproportionately the low-turnover ones. \citet{frazzini2018} estimate
real-world costs from live institutional trading and find them lower than the academic
consensus for large-cap strategies. Both literatures are calibrated to US market
microstructure. India's cost structure differs qualitatively---Securities Transaction Tax
on both legs, stamp duty on the buy alone, a flat depository fee on the sell---and to our
knowledge no open Indian equivalent exists.

\paragraph{Selective prediction.} The idea that a model should be allowed to abstain is
well developed in classification \citep{elyaniv2010,geifman2017}, where risk--coverage
curves are standard. We import the framework and change what it measures: coverage is the
fraction of \emph{strategies deployed}, and risk is realised out-of-sample Sharpe. The
translation is not free, and \S\ref{sec:design} discusses where it strains.

\paragraph{LLMs as code and research agents.} Code-generating models are now reliable
enough to produce syntactically valid, interface-conforming programs at scale
\citep{chen2021}, and finance-specific models exist \citep{wu2023}. What is not established
is whether the output constitutes research. Our corpus statistics
(\S\ref{sec:corpus}) are a direct measurement of that gap for one 7B open-weights model.

\section{Infrastructure}
\label{sec:infra}

Three components had to be built before any experiment could be run. Each is boring, and
each is load-bearing in the sense that a defect in it would silently invalidate everything
downstream. We describe them at the level of detail required to know whether to trust them.

\subsection{A survivorship-free, point-in-time universe}

The universe on date $t$ must contain exactly the securities investable on date $t$,
including those that later delisted. A universe built from today's index membership and
applied backwards embeds survivorship bias so thoroughly that no downstream correction can
remove it.

We did not use a published index membership list, because free point-in-time NIFTY~100
constituent history could not be sourced. Instead the universe is \emph{rules-based}: at
each quarterly rebalance, the 100 NSE equities with the highest trailing 126-session median
traded value, computed using only sessions strictly before the rebalance date, with a
buffer band to suppress rank churn. Nothing consults a membership list, so a company that
collapses simply stops qualifying rather than vanishing from history.

The 41 names that were in the first universe and absent from the last are the direct

Prices come from NSE's own end-of-day bhavcopy archives, adjusted for splits and bonuses.
We cross-checked against \texttt{yfinance} and found a \textbf{17.47\% disagreement rate on
adjusted closes} across 35,757 aligned observations. Inspection of the worst offenders
showed \emph{exact} integer ratios---BAJFINANCE at precisely $10\times$, ASHOKLEY at
precisely $2\times$---for securities with no declared corporate action in the window.
Genuine data noise is never exactly $10\times$. We designate the exchange file
authoritative and report the discrepancy rather than reconciling it, because the
reconciliation would be a fiction.

\subsection{An engine that makes lookahead hard to express}

Point-in-time discipline is enforced structurally rather than by convention. A strategy is
never handed the price panel; it receives a \texttt{MarketView} already truncated to before
its decision moment. Every signal carries an \texttt{information\_available\_at} stamp, and
the engine raises \texttt{PointInTimeError}---an exception, not a warning---if that stamp is
not strictly prior to the fill session. A signal formed at session $t$'s close fills at
session $t{+}1$'s \emph{open}.

Returns are accrued by provenance: a position carried from the previous session earns the
full close-to-close move including the overnight gap, while only the portion bought this
morning earns from the open. Collapsing both into open-to-close would silently liquidate
the book every evening, discarding most of the equity premium in the sample and making
every long-horizon strategy look structurally unprofitable.

\paragraph{Deliberate self-sabotage.} Three strategies that cheat on purpose are permanent
fixtures: one uses next-day return directly, one fits a \texttt{StandardScaler} on the full
sample before splitting, one selects its universe from end-of-period membership. Gross of
costs they return Sharpe ratios of \textbf{12{,}098}, \textbf{2.33} and \textbf{1.23}
respectively. Each holds its number when known data artifacts are removed, confirming it
cheats for its intended reason rather than riding a data scar. Thirty honest strategies
form the false-positive control. \emph{We built the disease before building the test for
it.}

As a sanity check, equal-weighting the universe returns 19.98\% annualised at 21.14\%
volatility over 1,232 sessions, with 7.64\% annualised tracking error and 0.9334
correlation against the NIFTY~100 price index---close enough to be a market proxy, distant
enough to reflect that it is an equal-weighted liquidity portfolio rather than a
capitalisation-weighted index.

\subsection{The Indian transaction cost model}
\label{sec:costmodel}

Costs are charged \emph{inside} the engine, per name and per side, at the statutory rates in
force on each trade's own date. The two legs are not symmetric---stamp duty falls on the buy
alone, the depository charge on the sell alone---so a blended round-trip rate gets both
wrong. Rates are stored as a schedule of complete epoch snapshots (three, reflecting the
April 2024 NSE reduction and the October 2024 SEBI true-to-label change) rather than as
deltas, so no edit can silently corrupt a neighbouring period.

Table~\ref{tab:cost} is pinned as a unit test, asserted to the paisa, so no future rate edit

\paragraph{The depository charge is not scale-invariant.} At \rupee15.34 per scrip per sell
instruction, a hundred-name book rebalancing once costs \rupee1,534 regardless of position
size---15 basis points of a \rupee10 lakh book, and 0.0015 basis points of a \rupee100 crore
one. Any conclusion about a diversified strategy is therefore partly a statement about
assumed capital. We report this dependence explicitly rather than hiding it in a default.

\section{The auditor}
\label{sec:auditor}

\subsection{$A_{\mathrm{static}}$: leakage by structure, not by syntax}

The static layer parses candidate source with Python's \texttt{ast} module and classifies
findings by leak class, line number, and severity. Its two strongest rules are structural
rather than syntactic: \emph{did the constructor accept bulk data?} and \emph{did the
decision function read stored state instead of the point-in-time view?} These mirror the
engine's own information boundary, which is why they catch cheats whose results look
plausible; pattern-matching \texttt{shift(-1)} catches only the cheats that announce
themselves.

Against the fixture suite the layer achieves recall $1.00$ (3/3 leaky) and precision
$1.00$ (0/30 honest flagged). \textbf{This number should be discounted and we say so in the
paper rather than the appendix.} With syntax-based detectors alone, recall was $1/3$; the
structural rules were added after observing that failure, and were then measured on the same
three fixtures that motivated them. That is closer to training accuracy than to a test. The
genuine out-of-sample evaluation is against generated code the detectors were never designed
around (\S\ref{sec:leakclasses}).

\subsection{$A_{\mathrm{stat}}$: deflation against an honest denominator}

We compute the Deflated Sharpe Ratio of \citet{bailey2014}, which converts an observed
Sharpe $\widehat{SR}$ into the probability that it exceeds what $N$ trials would produce by
luck, given sample length $T$, skewness $\gamma_3$ and kurtosis $\gamma_4$:
\[
\mathrm{DSR} \;=\; \Phi\!\left(
\frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR_0)\sqrt{T-1}}
     {\sqrt{1 - \gamma_3 \widehat{SR} + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^{\,2}}}
\right),
\]
where $SR_0$ is the expected maximum Sharpe across $N$ trials---a function of both $N$ and
the \emph{dispersion} of the trials' Sharpes. The mechanism deserves a plain statement,
because it is routinely misread: deflation does not shrink the strategy's Sharpe, it raises
the bar. With widely dispersed trials, 200 attempts buy an expected best of $2.77$ by luck
alone, so an observed $2.0$ is \emph{worse} than chance would produce.

This is worthless without an honest $N$, and an LLM generator is a machine for losing count.
We therefore maintain a \textbf{hash-chained, append-only trial ledger} outside the
generator's write path. Every draw increments it---including syntax errors, runtime
failures, and retries. Verification walks the chain and raises on any broken link, naming
the edited entry. Nothing under \texttt{src/generate/} opens the file.

\begin{selfreport}[title={One deliberate imperfection in the ledger}]
The ledger holds 1,877 entries but the true draw count is 1,887, because an early run recorded
one entry per candidate rather than one per draw. We did \textbf{not} retroactively edit the
chain to insert the missing ten. An append-only ledger that gets rewritten when found wrong is
not tamper-evident. The correction is applied at the point of use, and the discrepancy is
deliberate.
\end{selfreport}

\subsection{$A_{\mathrm{sem}}$: does the story match the code?}

The semantic layer presents \texttt{qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q4\_K\_M} with a candidate's stated
rationale and its implementation, and asks for one of four labels: \texttt{consistent},
\texttt{rationale\_\allowbreak implementation\_\allowbreak mismatch},
\texttt{unfalsifiable\_\allowbreak mechanism}, or
\texttt{unacknowledged\_\allowbreak known\_\allowbreak anomaly}. Validation is against 50
human-labelled items,
labelled before the model was run.

\textbf{Cohen's $\kappa = 0.589$} \citep{cohen1960}, raw agreement 38/50, at the top of the
``moderate'' band of \citet{landis1977}. Per-class agreement is uneven: \texttt{consistent}
26/31, \texttt{unfalsifiable\_mechanism} only 2/5. No prompt was tuned before this
measurement and none was tuned after it. The disposition---keep the layer---was decided
before the number was known.

One reconciliation is worth reporting because it is the ablation argument in miniature. The
human reviewer labelled \texttt{leak\_full\_sample\_scaler} as \texttt{consistent}, and that
is \emph{correct}: its rationale honestly describes what its code does, and its only defect
is where the scaler was fitted. It is caught by $A_{\mathrm{static}}$ and correctly passed by
$A_{\mathrm{sem}}$. \textbf{A leaky strategy can carry a perfectly honest rationale.} The
layers are not redundant, and any evaluation collapsing them into one ``flagged by
something'' signal would obscure that.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Generation.} One model, one prompt (SHA256 digest
\texttt{f307433c\ldots}), temperature 0.8, candidate $i$ seeded at $42+i$. 1,550 draws in two
batches, pooled only after asserting identical model tag, prompt digest, seed rule and
window. Each candidate emits a rationale, a feature specification, entry and exit rules, and
executable code conforming to the engine's strategy interface. Every attempt increments the
trial ledger.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Each candidate runs in a sandboxed worker with a 180-second bound
over the development window, net of costs, at a \rupee10 lakh book.

\paragraph{The abstention curve.} For coverage $c \in (0,1]$, rank candidates by auditor
confidence, retain the top $c$ fraction, and let $P(c)$ be the mean realised out-of-sample
Sharpe of the retained set. Then
\[
\mathrm{AUAP} \;=\; \int_0^1 P(c)\, \mathrm{d}c,
\]
evaluated on the grid $c \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 1.0\}$. The baseline is rejection at
random at matched coverage, with a bootstrap 95\% interval. \textbf{An auditor that is
informative must produce $P(c)$ rising as $c \to 0$.}

\paragraph{Two design choices that shape the result, stated plainly.} Flat candidates---those
that executed but never traded---are excluded from the ranking population rather than ranked
last. They are all tied at exactly zero and at 64\% of the executed set would dominate every
retained set. Ranking them last would have been equally defensible and would have produced a
different, lower curve. Second, survivors handed forward to future work are defined by
$A_{\mathrm{static}} \wedge A_{\mathrm{sem}}$, excluding the statistical layer, which rejects
the entire corpus and would make the handoff empty by construction. \textbf{This is the one
place we chose the definition that produces a non-empty answer, and we flag it as such.}

\paragraph{Holdout discipline.} The 2025 calendar year was frozen before any holdout data was
fetched, set to a calendar boundary rather than to the latest available session so that
window length could not become a researcher degree of freedom. It has been evaluated three times,
each under written authorisation logged with a timestamp: once on a gross-of-costs pipeline, once
after that pipeline was found defective and rebuilt, and once after the ranking defect described
in \S\ref{sec:correction}. The first evaluation is retired and its artifacts are frozen under a
directory whose README forbids quoting them without the word ``gross''; the second is superseded
and retained beside the third.

\textbf{Every one of the three was authorised in advance to repair a defect, and no parameter,
threshold, prompt or model was changed after any of them.} That is the property that matters, and
it is a different property from the count. A holdout is contaminated by iterative optimisation
against it, not by arithmetic being performed on it correctly more than once; but we recognise that
``we re-ran it and the conclusion held'' is exactly what a researcher would say in both the honest
and the dishonest case, so we publish the full before/after of each correction and the diffs that
produced them rather than asking to be believed. \textbf{The holdout is now permanently closed.}

\subsection{Leak classes, and one number we do not believe}
\label{sec:leakclasses}

Static analysis needs no execution, so it ran over all 1,550 sources: 222 rejected (14.3\%),
of which 195 carried one class, 26 two, and one three. By class:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lr@{\hspace{2.5em}}lr}
\toprule
\texttt{snooped\_parameter} & 166 & \texttt{boundary\_crossing\_window} & 2 \\
\texttt{future\_indexing} & 58 & \texttt{survivorship\_selection} & 1 \\
\texttt{target\_in\_features} & 23 & \texttt{full\_sample\_statistic} & \textbf{0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{We do not believe the zero.} Full-sample statistics are the most common leak class in
hand-written quantitative code, and a corpus of 1,550 candidates containing none of it is not
plausible. The cause is almost certainly ours: to raise the executable rate from near zero,
the prompt advises working in plain Python rather than in long polars expression chains, and
\texttt{pl.col("x").mean()} over an unfiltered frame is precisely where this class arises.
The class distribution above therefore describes \emph{this prompt}, not LLM-generated
strategies in general, and the zero should be read as evidence that the instrument is blind
here---not as evidence the defect is absent.

\paragraph{Most leak flags land on code that never produced a result.} Static analysis is a
statement about source, not about realised PnL, and the two are correctly independent: a leak is
a leak whether or not the strategy went on to trade. But the \emph{distribution} of where flags
land is itself a measurement, and on this corpus it is stark.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Fate of the 222 static-rejected candidates} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Share} \\
\midrule
Runtime error --- never ran & 114 & 51.4\% \\
Evaluated but flat --- ran, never traded & 79 & 35.6\% \\
Timeout & 1 & 0.5\% \\
\textbf{Evaluated and traded} & \textbf{28} & \textbf{12.6\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{Only 28 leak findings in the entire corpus attach to a strategy that took a
position.} A worked example makes the mechanism concrete. Candidate 053 is flagged
\texttt{future\_indexing} at confidence 1.0 for a \texttt{shift(-1)} whose result is then filtered
onto a past session date---textbook future indexing, and correctly caught. But the block is
unreachable: the guard preceding it requires 21 rows from a view that returns at most 20, so it is
false on every session and the function exits before the leak executes. Had it been reached it
would have raised twice over, on an invalid \texttt{date} construction and on a column absent from
the pivoted frame.

The verdict and the flat return series are compatible, not contradictory. What the pairing shows
is that \textbf{a leakage prevalence rate measured over generated source is not a deployment risk
rate}. Reported as ``14.3\% of candidates leak'', the static layer sounds like it is intercepting
a substantial hazard; reported as ``28 of 1,550 candidates leak \emph{and} trade'', the same
measurement says the hazard is rare because the population is inert. Both numbers are true. Only
the second is decision-relevant, and we are not aware of prior work that separates them.

\medskip
The semantic layer labelled 90.1\% of the corpus \texttt{consistent}, rejecting 154 (9.9\%):
119 rationale--implementation mismatches, 21 unacknowledged known anomalies, 14 unfalsifiable
mechanisms. It is not a detector that fires indiscriminately. Whether the 154 are the
\emph{right} ones is not established on this population, since $\kappa$ was measured on a
different one.

\subsection{Deflation at an honest trial count}
\label{sec:deflation}

At $N = 1{,}887$, the deflated Sharpe probability exceeds 0.95 for \textbf{0 of 631}
evaluated candidates. The corpus maximum is $4.4 \times 10^{-8}$. The best raw Sharpe in the
corpus, 1.3049, carries a deflated probability of $5.9\times10^{-5}$.

Those two numbers should feel violently inconsistent, and the inconsistency is the point:
apparent skill and statistical significance are different quantities, and the gap between
them is exactly what a trial count of 1,887 buys.

\paragraph{Result: the statistical layer's rejection rate is uninterpretable at this trial
count.} We state this as a finding rather than a caveat, because it is an empirical observation
about the instrument and not a hedge about the data.

\keyfinding{\textbf{At $N = 1{,}887$ over 1,232 sessions, every evaluated strategy is rejected.
Whether this reflects appropriate statistical correction or saturation of the test cannot be
determined from these data.} The deflation term grows with $N$ while sample length is fixed, so
at some ratio the test rejects everything regardless of merit. A 100\% rejection rate is
consistent with an honest correction \emph{and} with a saturated one, and no measurement in this
study separates them.}

\noindent This bears directly on the ablation in \S\ref{sec:auap}, and more sharply than we first
realised. The layer rejects all 631 strategies it scores, at a recorded confidence of exactly
$1.0$ in every case, so it carries \emph{no} ordering information whatever among the candidates it
scored: they all tie. Its only discrimination is between scored and unscored. The four
configurations containing it are the four with the steepest fall at tight coverage. The rejection
rate and the ranking failure are plausibly the same fact observed twice. Establishing which requires evaluating the layer across trial-count regimes, which this
corpus cannot support because $N$ is a property of the experiment rather than a free parameter.

The Probability of Backtest Overfitting could not be computed on the pooled corpus: the
eleven ruined strategies produce short series and the common-length matrix loses too many
columns. \textbf{We report it as unavailable rather than repairing it by dropping the ruined
strategies}, which would be a post-hoc restriction not part of the intended protocol. On the
twelve-fixture suite, where it can be computed, PBO is 0.588 (0.531--0.667 across split
counts)---above 0.5 in every variant, meaning selecting the in-sample best yields a
below-median strategy out of sample more often than not.

\textbf{No configuration beats random rejection at matched coverage}
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:auap}). One sits inside the random interval; six sit
below it, meaning they order strategies \emph{worse} than chance. The null is not rejected.

Figure~\ref{fig:auap} makes a point the table cannot. The curves do not merely fail to rise as
coverage tightens---all of them \emph{fall}. Concentrating on the candidates the auditor trusts
most selects a worse portfolio than taking everything. Whatever the layers are ranking on, it is
not out-of-sample performance, and at high confidence it is mildly anti-correlated with it.

One diagnostic is worth the space. On development data, the \texttt{statistical} layer alone
was the only configuration to beat random---and it provably cannot be evidence, because its
confidence is $1 - \mathrm{DSR}$ and DSR is a monotone function of the very development
Sharpe that $P(c)$ measures. Ranking by a quantity and then scoring on that same quantity
beats random by construction. Out of sample the comparison becomes legitimate, and the layer
fails with the rest, landing fourth of seven and below the random interval, with the worst
$P(0.05)$ of any single layer. \textbf{The circularity diagnosis was made by argument in advance
and confirmed by experiment afterwards.}

The corpus also collapses wholesale from development to holdout: mean Sharpe $+{\sim}0.40$
gross in sample against $-1.0351$ net out of sample, with 216/221 positive in sample and
roughly half positive out of it. The best out-of-sample performer, at $+1.3618$, had a
development Sharpe of $-1.6696$. \textbf{The single best out-of-sample strategy is one any
sane process would have discarded in sample.} That is not a finding about the strategy. It is
multiple testing appearing inside our own results, which is the most direct evidence we can
offer that the phenomenon we set out to measure is real.

\subsection{A sign error in our own ranking, and what it cost}
\label{sec:correction}

The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} are the second version. The first inverted the semantic
layer's contribution, and we describe the defect in full because a paper about detecting false
results that concealed one of its own would be self-refuting.

Each auditor layer records a \texttt{confidence} alongside its verdict. The code that turned those
into a ranking negated the confidence, on the assumption that it meant \emph{confidence that
something is wrong}. For the static layer it does---the field holds a finding count. For the
statistical layer it does vacuously, since that layer rejects every candidate it scores at
confidence $1.0$. For the semantic layer it does not: the language model reports confidence in
\emph{its own label}, around $0.95$ for a confident \texttt{consistent} and $0.85$ for a confident
defect. Negating it ranked the strategies that layer had condemned above the ones it had cleared.
The Spearman correlation between the two rankings is $-0.972$, and the set retained at 5\% coverage
went from eleven of eleven semantically rejected to zero of eleven.

Four of the seven configurations were affected. The other three are provably untouched: under the
corrected rule the static and statistical layers produce numerically identical scores on this
corpus, and the recomputed AUAP for all three agrees to every printed digit. Nothing else was
changed---not the corpus, the labels, the backtests, the cost model, the trial counter, or the
holdout contents.

\paragraph{What the defect did not do.} It did not change the conclusion, and it did not flatter
us. Every affected AUAP moved \emph{down}---the best configuration went from $-1.1441$ to
$-1.1967$---and the identity of the best configuration did not change. The inverted ranking had
been the more favourable one.

\paragraph{A second error, found while checking the first.} An earlier draft of this section
reported that the correction raised the number of configurations ordering strategies worse than
chance from two to six. It did not. Six of the seven AUAPs were already below the random
interval before the correction; the sentence counting them in the previous version of this paper
said ``two'', and its table annotated \texttt{static} at $-1.2529$ as below the interval while
leaving \texttt{statistical} at $-1.3497$ unmarked. That was a transcription error in the prose,
not in the measurement, and it is corrected here rather than folded into the ranking correction it
did not belong to. The count is six of seven in both versions.

\paragraph{What it did do, and why it belongs in the results.} On development data the correction
raises $P(0.05)$ for the semantic layer from $-0.1088$ to $+0.2910$, and for
\texttt{static + semantic} from $-0.0918$ to $+0.3142$, against a population mean of $-0.1896$. A
correctly-oriented semantic layer visibly concentrates the better strategies at tight coverage
\emph{on the data it was developed against}. The identical ranking on the holdout gives $-1.1347$
and $-1.1805$. The gap between an in-sample selectivity that looks real and an out-of-sample one
that is absent is this paper's subject, and it appeared here by accident, in our own pipeline,
while we were measuring it in someone else's.

\paragraph{Why no test caught it.} Each layer's tests check its labels, which were right. The
abstention tests check that a curve is computed correctly from a ranking, which it was. The defect
lived only in the join: a ranking computed correctly from a quantity that meant the opposite of
what the calling code believed. It was found not by testing but by attempting to reuse the ranking
for a different purpose in a subsequent project---which is a general lesson about where this class
of defect hides, and a limitation of static and unit-level checking that our own static layer
shares. A regression test now asserts the join directly.

\section{Negative results}
\label{sec:negative}

This section exists because the charter governing this work requires it, and because a paper
that reports only what worked is the artifact this paper is about.

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0.35em}
\item \textbf{$\mathrm{AUAP}$ does not beat random rejection}, in seven of seven
  configurations, out of sample, under a pre-registered protocol. Six configurations are
  worse than chance.
\item \textbf{The null survived two full corrections of our own pipeline.} The first
  evaluation was gross of costs because no cost model existed. We built one, wired it in, and
  re-ran everything downstream. The magnitudes moved substantially---holdout mean from
  $-0.2755$ to $-1.0351$---and the conclusion did not move at all. The second correction is
  described in \S\ref{sec:correction}: a sign error that had inverted the semantic layer's
  contribution to the ranking. Every affected number moved, and the conclusion again did not.
  We consider this the most informative single fact in the paper, because it distinguishes
  ``we found a null'' from ``we found a null and then twice checked whether it was an artifact
  of our own defect.''
\item \textbf{This null is confounded with corpus degeneracy}, and we cannot resolve the
  confound. The 225 rankable strategies are tightly clustered and mostly weak. A null
  therefore cannot distinguish \emph{``the auditor is uninformative''} from \emph{``this
  corpus is too weak to test the auditor''}. Both explanations survive the data. This caveat
  travels with the number permanently, and it is the reason the benchmark is released.
\item \textbf{The tracking-error decomposition failed.} Total tracking error against the
  index is 0.0775 over 1,231 sessions, but the two legs of the attempted decomposition came
  out at 0.1152 and 0.1463---each exceeding the total they were meant to partition. The
  traded-value proxy does not stand in for capitalisation weighting. Only the total is
  reported. Nothing was adjusted to make the numbers add up.
\item \textbf{The one suggestive signal we reported did not survive our own correction, and we
  record that it existed.} Before the ranking defect of \S\ref{sec:correction} was found, the
  semantic layer alone reached $P(0.05) = -0.5147$ against a population mean of $-1.0351$---its
  most confident 5\% appearing to do roughly twice as well as average. We declined to build on it
  at the time, on the grounds that five per cent of 225 is eleven strategies and the random
  interval's width said it was noise. It was worse than noise: it was the inverted ranking, and
  those eleven strategies were the ones the layer had \emph{rejected}. Corrected, the same figure
  is $-1.1347$, slightly \emph{below} the population mean. \textbf{The suggestive signal is gone
  and nothing replaced it.} We report the episode rather than the corrected number alone, because
  the useful part is that a caveat stated in the right direction still understated the problem.
\item \textbf{27 of the 174 surviving strategies are not deterministic functions of their
  inputs.} Run one again, on the same panel with the same code, and its Sharpe changes---by up to
  $2.5352$. Two mechanisms, both confirmed in source: unseeded randomness in 26 of the 27, and
  \texttt{set} iteration order, which Python randomises per process, in 27 of 27. The clearest
  instance keeps an arbitrary ten of its qualifying symbols via
  \texttt{list(set(\ldots))[:10]}, giving Sharpe $0.7652$, $0.4566$ and $0.6825$ under three hash
  seeds. All 27 are generator output; all eleven hand-written factor fixtures are deterministic,
  which locates the defect in what the model wrote rather than in the harness that ran it.
  Survivor membership is unaffected---the auditors read source, and the only performance gate is a
  flatness threshold seven orders of magnitude away---and the null is unaffected, because noise
  makes an ordering harder to detect and cannot manufacture one. The $\mathrm{AUAP}$ figures are
  computed from these Sharpe ratios and carry the noise, so their trailing digits are not exact.
  \textbf{We report this as a property of LLM-generated strategies that the literature does not
  discuss: a generated strategy may not define a portfolio as a function of data at all}, and no
  auditor layer we built detects it, because it is invisible in a single execution.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{selfreport}[title={A process failure we are obliged to report}]
During the cost re-run, six candidates were silently reclassified as runtime errors by a
type-inference defect: summing over an empty book returns integer zero, which caused a column to
be inferred as integer and raise on the first genuine float. \textbf{The exit code was zero.}
Among the six was the candidate holding the highest holdout Sharpe in the previous evaluation.

It was caught only by comparing funnel counts against the retired gross run---a check imposed by
review, not by the code. \textbf{Without that comparison this paper would have been wrong and
would have looked fine.} We report it because a paper about detecting silent failure that
concealed its own would be self-refuting.
\end{selfreport}

\begin{selfreport}[title={A second one, found after the benchmark was frozen}]
\texttt{PYTHONHASHSEED} is set nowhere in our repository, so the entire corpus was backtested with
hash randomisation active and a different seed in every worker process. Our seeding module seeds
Python's \texttt{random} and NumPy; neither reaches a strategy that constructs its own generator,
and neither can affect hash order, which must be fixed before the interpreter starts. Every auditor
layer had tests. \textbf{No test ran the same strategy twice and compared the results.}

The gap survived all of Phase~1 and was found only when the follow-on project needed a variance
across resampled histories to mean something---at which point a strategy whose score already varies
for no reason becomes arithmetically indistinguishable from the quantity being measured. The check
that would have caught it is one line. It was the absence of the check, rather than any individual
script, that failed, and the charter we wrote for ourselves forbids exactly this.
\end{selfreport}

\begin{selfreport}[title={A third, and the one that bears on the null}]
Of 150 corpus strategies compared in follow-on work, \textbf{11 clusters contain strategies whose
realised net return series are identical on every session} to within $10^{-12}$: different code,
different stated rationale, identical behaviour. 27 strategies sit in a cluster; the largest holds
four. A further 20 pairs correlate above 0.9999.

\textbf{One cluster pairs a generated candidate with the equal-weight baseline it was meant to
beat.} The model rediscovered equal-weighting and presented it as novel with its own economic
rationale. $A_{\mathrm{sem}}$ is specified to flag exactly this---``unacknowledged known
anomaly''---and did not. That is one checkable miss found incidentally, not a measured
false-negative rate, and we do not present it as one.

The generator's failure mode is therefore not only that its strategies are weak but that
\textbf{it writes the same weak strategy repeatedly}, and no auditor layer can see this, because
every layer reads one candidate at a time. This is direct evidence for the corpus-degeneracy
confound we flag against our own null in \S\ref{sec:auap}: a corpus with 27 behavioural
duplicates in 150 has fewer effective candidates than its count implies, so an abstention curve
computed over it ranks fewer distinct objects than the coverage axis suggests. The null stands as
reported. The confound is now measured rather than suspected.
\end{selfreport}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0.3em}
\item \textbf{One generator, one prompt, one temperature.} Every corpus statistic describes
  \texttt{qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q4\_K\_M} at temperature 0.8 under a single prompt whose digest
  is published. These are not claims about LLM strategy generation in general. A stronger
  generator would plausibly change the funnel and might change the null.
\item \textbf{The prompt steers implementation style}, which biases the leak-class
  distribution downward in at least one class (\S\ref{sec:leakclasses}).
\item \textbf{The corpus is smaller than its count.} 27 of 150 compared strategies are exact
  behavioural duplicates of another, so the effective number of distinct candidates the abstention
  curve ranks is lower than the coverage axis implies, by an amount we have not corrected for. No
  auditor layer detects this, because each reads one candidate in isolation. Cross-candidate
  redundancy detection is an obvious addition to the auditor and we did not build it.
\item \textbf{The static layer's perfect fixture score is provisional}, for the reason given
  in \S\ref{sec:auditor}: its decisive rules were written after seeing it fail.
\item \textbf{Slippage and impact coefficients are uncalibrated.} They account for roughly
  16\% of drag on realistically-trading strategies at a \rupee10 lakh book, but they dominate
  in the large-book regime, so Table~\ref{tab:dp}'s right-hand rows are more assumption-laden
  than its left-hand ones.
\item \textbf{Three historical statutory rates could not be sourced} and are held constant
  across the window; each is documented with the maximum error it could introduce, jointly
  under one part in a thousand of a round trip. Stamp duty before July 2020 was
  state-dependent and the uniform rate overstates cost in the first six months.
\item \textbf{The universe is a liquidity proxy, not the NIFTY~100}, because free
  point-in-time index membership could not be obtained. It is survivorship-free by
  construction, which was the binding requirement, but it is not the index and is never
  described as such.
\item \textbf{The statistical layer was evaluated at a single trial count.} Future work should
  evaluate it across different trial-count regimes; the result at $N = 1{,}887$ is reported in
  \S\ref{sec:deflation} rather than here, because it is an observation and not a caveat.
\item \textbf{PBO is unavailable} on the pooled corpus, for the reason in
  \S\ref{sec:deflation}.
\item \textbf{Flat candidates are excluded from the ranking population} rather than ranked
  last; the alternative is defensible and would lower the curve.
\item \textbf{The holdout is a single calendar year} (246 sessions) of one market. Out-of-sample
  evidence of this length supports a direction, not a magnitude.
\item \textbf{Reported performance for 27 survivors is one draw, not a fixed quantity}, for the
  reason in \S\ref{sec:negative}. We did not re-run under a pinned seed, and would not: pinning
  replaces one arbitrary draw with another rather than with a correct value, because the defect is
  in the strategies and not in our measurement of them. Any downstream use of the surviving set
  should exclude them---and must report that doing so is not performance-neutral, since the
  excluded 27 average $-0.6722$ against the retained 147's $-0.1320$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Contents} \\
\midrule

The protocol is deliberately restrictive: a fixed shared holdout, \textbf{one evaluation per
submission}, no tuning on holdout results, and no revision of the benchmark in response to
submissions. Independent one-shot evaluations do not contaminate a frozen test set; iterative
optimisation against it does, and a benchmark that permits the latter measures nothing after its
first few users. Every reported AUAP must carry its trial count $N$ and its generator, because
an AUAP without the trial count that produced its corpus is not interpretable.

\paragraph{Availability.} Everything in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} is at
\href{https://github.com/Ashwin-aadi/Trivijaya-Quant-Research}%
{\texttt{github.com/Ashwin-aadi/Trivijaya-Quant-Research}}, together with the source of this
paper. Start at \texttt{benchmarks/alphaaudit/RESULTS.md}, which carries every number in this
study with its sample size, including the ones we could not compute and the reason why.

\paragraph{What a submission must beat.} The reference entry is the seven-configuration table in
\S\ref{sec:auap}: best AUAP $-1.1967$, against a random-rejection interval of $[-1.2208,
-0.8600]$, on a corpus generated by \texttt{qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q4\_K\_M} at $N = 1{,}887$. This
is a low bar. We publish it as one.

\paragraph{The honest reason a benchmark is the right artifact here.} Our null is confounded
with corpus degeneracy and we cannot resolve the confound (\S\ref{sec:negative}). A single group
with a single 7B generator cannot. What can resolve it is a second entry using a stronger
generator on the identical environment: if a capable generator produces a corpus on which some
auditor beats random, the degeneracy explanation is settled and our null is correctly read as a
statement about weak corpora. The benchmark exists because that is the experiment we cannot run
alone.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to determine whether an adversarial auditor can tell real trading signals from
artifacts in a corpus written by a language model. On the evidence assembled here, for this
generator, it cannot---not better than chance.

Three things were learned along the way that we did not expect to be the interesting part.
The dominant failure mode of an LLM strategy researcher is not dishonesty but inertness:
six candidates in ten never survive contact with data, and two thirds of the rest never
trade.
Indian transaction costs, honestly modelled, reverse the sign of one profitable strategy in
six and reduce the momentum premium on a liquid universe to the width of the noise. And an
honest trial count of 1,887 removes every trace of statistical significance from a corpus
whose best member has a superficially respectable Sharpe of 1.28.

The infrastructure functions as designed, and it behaved consistently under repeated
evaluation: it reported no signal because none was detectable in this corpus, and it reported
the same thing after we corrected our own most serious implementation error. That invariance
is the closest thing to a control a null result admits.

What we are left holding is therefore not a method but a measuring apparatus, and that is what
we release. A benchmark whose reference implementation fails its own headline test is an
unusual object to publish, but it is the honest one: the protocol, the fixtures, the corpus and
the cost model are all independent of whether our particular auditor worked, and they are what
another group would otherwise have to rebuild before disagreeing with us. It is all at
\href{https://github.com/Ashwin-aadi/Trivijaya-Quant-Research}%
{\texttt{github.com/Ashwin-aadi/Trivijaya-Quant-Research}}.
